Flow Pattern Interpretation

The largest receiving locations are concentrated in the Midwest and parts of the South, where the darkest choropleth shading indicates the highest Total Flow (1887–1924) values. States such as those in the central U.S. show large node sizes (high net inflow), meaning they acted as major hubs within the family‑tree network. In contrast, several sending regions appear along the eastern and southeastern United States, where node sizes are smaller or negative (net outflow). These states have lighter choropleth shading and smaller circles, indicating that they contributed more individuals to the network than they received.

The dominant flow lines reveal a strong east‑to‑west and southeast‑to‑midwest movement pattern. Many of the thickest flow lines curve from the Atlantic coastal states toward the central interior, reflecting historical population shifts during the late 19th and early 20th centuries. There is also a noticeable south‑to‑north component, with flows moving from the Gulf region toward the Midwest. These directional patterns align with broader U.S. migration trends of the period, including agricultural transitions, industrialization, and expanding transportation networks.

The choropleth layer (Total Flow) and node symbols (Net Flow) reinforce the same spatial story: Dark blue states (high total flow) correspond to large node circles, showing that these areas were both major receivers and central connectors in the network. Lighter green states with small or negative net‑flow nodes represent regions that sent out more genealogical connections than they received. The thickest flow lines originate from high‑sending states and terminate in high‑receiving states, visually linking the choropleth and node patterns. Together, the symbology shows a coherent structure: the Midwest functioned as a major attractor and redistribution hub, while the eastern and southeastern states acted as sources feeding into the broader U.S. family‑tree network.
